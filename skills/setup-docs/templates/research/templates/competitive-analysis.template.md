# Competitive Analysis: <Market/Product Area>

## Scope
What market or product area is being compared, and why now?

- Name the specific product surface being compared — "project management tools" is too broad to yield an actionable comparison; "async task assignment for 5-20 person teams" is scoped enough to pick real competitors.
- Say what triggered this analysis now — a competitor launch, a pricing change, a sales-loss pattern, a planning cycle — since that shapes which competitors and features matter most.
- Decide upfront whether you're comparing against direct competitors, adjacent substitutes, or both; conflating them muddies the feature comparison table below.

## Competitors evaluated
| Competitor | Positioning | Strengths | Weaknesses | Pricing |
|---|---|---|---|---|

- Include only competitors a prospect would actually compare you against — padding the table with irrelevant players dilutes the analysis.
- "Positioning" is how they describe themselves (their own words/tagline), not your opinion of them — save judgment for Strengths/Weaknesses.
- Weaknesses should be things that matter to a buyer, not nitpicks — a weakness nobody in the target segment cares about isn't a real opportunity.

## Feature comparison
| Capability | Us | Competitor A | Competitor B |
|---|---|---|---|

- Pick capabilities buyers actually evaluate on, not every feature either product has — a table with 40 rows tells a reader less than one with 8 well-chosen rows.
- Use consistent values per cell (yes/no/partial, or a one-line note) so the table is scannable at a glance rather than requiring a close read of each cell.
- Verify each row against the competitor's current docs/product, not memory or an old comparison someone forwarded you — this table is exactly what goes stale first.

## Differentiation opportunities
Where can we win that competitors can't easily match?

- Ground each opportunity in a weakness or gap identified above — an opportunity with no corresponding row in the tables is speculation, not analysis.
- Distinguish opportunities that are structural (a competitor's business model or architecture makes it hard for them to follow) from ones that are just a head start (they could ship it next quarter if they chose to).

*A differentiator only counts if it's also something the target segment actually values — "we're the only one with feature X" isn't an opportunity if nobody asked for X.*

## Risks & threats
Where are competitors ahead, or where could one move fast enough to close a gap we're counting on?

- Call out gaps where a competitor is already ahead today, not just hypothetical future moves — those are the most urgent to plan around.
- For each threat, note how fast it could materialize — a well-funded competitor closing a gap in one release cycle is a different risk than a slow-moving incumbent.
- Consider new entrants and adjacent players, not just the competitors already in the table above — the biggest threat to a market is sometimes not on anyone's competitor list yet.

## Sources
Links/dates for anything time-sensitive (pricing, feature lists) — competitive info goes stale fast.

- Date every source, not just link it — a pricing page cited without a date is unverifiable six months from now.
- Note anything gathered informally (a sales call, a customer's offhand comment about a competitor) separately from public sources, since it carries different confidence.
