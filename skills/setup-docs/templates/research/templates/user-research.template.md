# User Research: <Study Name>

## Research question
What did we want to learn, and what decision does it inform?

- Phrase it as something you didn't already know the answer to — a research question you could answer without talking to users isn't worth a study.
- Name the decision it feeds, even loosely (a feature bet, a redesign, a prioritization call) — research with no attached decision tends not to get read once it's done.
- If the question narrowed or shifted once recruiting or sessions started, note the version you actually studied, not just the original framing.

## Method
Interviews, surveys, usability testing, diary study, etc. — including sample size and recruitment criteria.

- Name the actual method and format (moderated vs. unmoderated, remote vs. in-person, structured vs. open-ended) — "interviews" alone doesn't tell a reader how to weigh the findings.
- State the sample size plainly; a finding from 5 interviews should be read differently than one from a 200-person survey, and the reader can only do that if you say the number.
- If there's a companion interview guide or discussion script, link it here instead of restating the question set.

## Participants
Who took part (segment, count, how recruited)?

- Describe the segment in terms relevant to the research question (role, tenure, usage frequency), not just demographics that don't affect the findings.
- Say how they were recruited (customer list, panel, guerrilla intercept) — recruitment method is a major source of bias and belongs in the open, not buried.
- Note anyone who dropped out, no-showed, or was screened out and why — a skewed final sample changes how much weight the findings can bear.

## Findings
Grouped by theme, not by participant. Cite direct quotes/evidence where they support a finding.

- Group by theme or pattern across participants — a findings section organized "Participant 1 said... Participant 2 said..." makes the reader do the synthesis you were supposed to do.
- Attach a quote, screen-recording timestamp, or specific behavior to each finding — an unsupported finding is indistinguishable from an assumption.
- Note how many participants a pattern held for (e.g. "4 of 6") so a reader can tell a near-universal finding from an outlier one person raised.

## Implications
What should this change about product/design decisions? Link to the PRD or flow-spec this feeds into, if any.

- Tie each implication back to a specific finding above — an implication with no traceable finding behind it is opinion dressed as research output.
- Be explicit about confidence: a finding backed by 8 of 10 participants supports a stronger claim than one backed by an offhand comment from one person.

## Follow-up questions
What's still unanswered, or what would the next round of research need to cover?

- List questions this study raised but couldn't answer, not just things that would be "nice to know" — a good follow-up list saves the next researcher from re-deriving scope from scratch.
- This report is a snapshot of what participants said at the time of the study — if you need updated answers later, that's a new study, not a revision to this one.
