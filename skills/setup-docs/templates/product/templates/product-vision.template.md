# Product Vision: <Product Name>

## Vision statement
One or two sentences: what does this product make true for its users, long-term?

- Write it to still be true in three years — if it references a specific feature or current competitor, it's a roadmap item, not a vision.
- It should be aspirational but falsifiable — someone should be able to look at the product later and judge whether it's living up to this or not.

## Who it's for
The core audience this vision is built around.

- Name who this is emphatically *not* for as much as who it is for — a vision that's for everyone tends to guide no decisions.
- This can be broader than any single persona; it's the population the whole product strategy is built around, not one segment's needs.

## Why now
What makes this the right time to pursue this vision?

- Point to something that changed — a market shift, a technology becoming viable, a capability the team now has — not just "we think it's a good idea."
- *If the honest answer is "no particular reason," that's worth sitting with — a vision without a "why now" is easier to deprioritize the moment something else looks urgent.*

## What success looks like
Directionally — not a specific metric target (that belongs in an OKR), but what the world looks like if this vision is realized.

- Describe the user's world, not the company's — what can they do, feel, or stop worrying about that they can't today?
- Avoid smuggling in a metric here; if you're writing a percentage, it belongs in an OKR that operationalizes this vision instead.

## What this is not
Explicit boundaries — adjacent problems this product deliberately does not try to solve.

- Name the adjacent products or problems people will assume are included because they're related — this is what keeps the vision from being reinterpreted as "solve everything nearby."
- Revisit this occasionally: a boundary that made sense at founding may deserve a deliberate decision to keep or lift later, not just drift.
