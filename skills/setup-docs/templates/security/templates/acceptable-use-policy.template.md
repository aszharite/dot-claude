# Acceptable Use Policy: <Organization/System Name>

## Scope
What systems, devices, and accounts this policy covers.

- Cover both company-owned and personal devices used for work (BYOD) — a policy silent on personal phones checking company email has a hole exactly where many incidents start.
- Name who it applies to — employees, contractors, interns — since contract terms sometimes assume a separate agreement covers this and it doesn't.

## Permitted use
What's explicitly allowed.

- Cover the common ambiguous cases directly (occasional personal use of a work laptop, personal accounts on a managed device) — silence here gets read as either "allowed" or "forbidden" depending who's asking, which is the problem.
- Keep this to what's actually allowed, not a restatement of prohibited use in positive language — redundant sections drift out of sync with each other over time.

## Prohibited use
What's explicitly disallowed (e.g. unauthorized software, sharing credentials, unapproved removable media).

- Name the specific behaviors that actually cause incidents at this org (shared passwords, personal cloud storage for work files, disabling endpoint security) — a generic list copied from elsewhere misses what's actually risky here.
- Distinguish "prohibited" from "requires approval" — some things (installing new software, using a personal device) may be conditionally fine with sign-off rather than outright banned.

## Device & account requirements
Password/passphrase requirements, MFA, device encryption, screen lock.

- State concrete, checkable requirements (minimum passphrase length, MFA method required, encryption standard) — "strong passwords" isn't enforceable or auditable.
- Cover what happens when a device is lost or stolen — remote wipe capability, reporting timeframe — since that's the requirement most likely to matter under actual pressure.

## Enforcement
Consequences of violation, and how violations are reported.

- Name the actual consequence range (warning, access revocation, termination) tied to severity — a policy with no stated consequence is optional in practice.
- State who a violation gets reported to and how — an enforcement section with no reporting path just describes penalties nobody ever triggers.
